# Messaging House

Most product copy fails for one reason: it lists what the product does instead of saying why anyone should care. Strategyzer's Value Proposition Design fixes the thinking but stops at a canvas. This skill carries the thinking through to a work product: a messaging house that every channel draws on, and copy that traces back to a job a real customer is trying to get done.

## Where the method comes from

At Microsoft, Surface launches ran on a messaging house: four why-buy reasons across the top, and the specs coming out of engineering stacked in columns beneath them. The columns were negotiated in both directions. Sometimes a why-buy had to bend to fit what the product really did; sometimes the specs were reshuffled until the columns held. After leaving Microsoft and NVIDIA to start a consulting agency, Casey Milone became certified in Strategyzer's Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Design and saw that the canvas's fit map, features against pain relievers and gain creators, was the missing way to derive those four why-buys instead of guessing them. The method since then: a value proposition workshop, four why-buys, sessions with product and engineering to stack the specs, and a four-blade homepage as the finished artefact. The point of the whole thing is that strategy work ends in something identifiable you can publish, not a deck that sits in a drawer.

## How to run this skill with a person

Assume the user is coming in cold: no marketing background, no canvas, possibly no clear idea who their customer is. That is the normal case and the skill is built for it. Follow these habits throughout.

Open by telling them what this is and what they will walk away with: a one-page messaging house, a value proposition, and copy for the channel they need, all derived from one real customer. Two or three sentences, then move.

Ask for the inputs you need, one question at a time, and only for what they have not already given you. The inputs are: what the product is (a spec sheet, feature list, or URL is ideal); one real customer; what that customer used before; and any brand voice rules. A stranger can answer one question; a questionnaire makes them close the tab.

Show your work at each stage and wait for a yes before building on it. The stages are cheap to redo early and expensive to redo late; a wrong hero customer at stage 1 makes everything after it wrong.

Name the work product in plain terms when you deliver it, and say what to do with it next.

Fast track: if the user already has the inputs, has run the skill before, or says they know what they want, skip the explainers and the stage-by-stage confirmations, run the chain end to end, and put your assumptions at the top of the output for them to correct. Offer the fast track when you can see they are experienced; do not force the slow path on someone who does not need it. The same applies when you are running non-interactively (for example, a task with no one to ask): proceed on the best reading of the brief and state assumptions.

There are two moments where the interview matters most. Do not skip them on the slow path.

Interview one, at stage 1: pin down one real customer and get the raw spec list out of the user. This is the conversation you would have with the engineering team.

Interview two, at stage 3: show the four candidate why-buys and ask the user to argue with them before anything is stacked underneath. This is where the room used to push post-its around.

## The chain

1. Customer profile: one real customer; their jobs, pains and gains.
2. Value map: how the product answers each job, pain and gain, and how well.
3. Four why-buys: the four reasons to buy, derived from the tightest fits.
4. Messaging house: every feature and spec stacked under the why-buy it proves. The single source of truth.
5. Value proposition and why-buy copy: the prose that channels reuse.
6. Channel copy: the four-blade homepage first; PR, ads, email, sales material as asked.

Read `references/value-proposition-canvas.md` before stage 1 for the jobs-to-be-done explainer and the interview questions. Read `references/messaging-house-method.md` before stage 3 for the why-buy tests, the two-way fit rule, and the worked example. Read `references/channel-assets.md` before stage 6.

## Stage 1: one real customer, and what they are trying to get done

Jobs to be done is the foundation the whole method stands on, so make sure the user understands it before they answer anything. Give them this, in your own words and briefly: pick one real person who uses the product, not a segment or a demographic. Think about what that person is trying to get done at the moment they reach for the product. Some jobs are practical (get the invoice out), some are social (look organised to a client), some are emotional (stop worrying about cash flow at night). Customers are bad at saying what they want, but they are good at describing what they were trying to do, so ask about the doing. The competition is whatever else gets hired for the same job, which is often not another product in the same category.

Then ask the first question: "Tell me about one real customer, by name if you can, and what they were doing right before they used your product." If no name is given, or you are on the fast track, invent a first name and say it is a composite; a profile written for "the founder" or "the user" drifts back into a segment within a page. Draft the jobs list from the answer, eight to twelve items covering practical, social and emotional jobs, ranked by how much the customer would pay to make the job go away. Show it back and ask what is wrong with it.

Pains next: what makes those jobs frustrating, risky or embarrassing, what the customer fears, what mistakes they make with what they use today. Gains after that: how the customer measures a win, what they would tell a colleague about. Eight to twelve each, ranked, shown back. The questions in the reference file help when a list is thin.

Collect the spec list in this stage too, if you do not have it. Every line of it will land in the house later, so ask for the boring engineering version, not the marketing version.

## Stage 2: value map and fit

Mirror the profile. Products and services answer jobs; pain relievers answer pains; gain creators answer gains. Write each answer against the profile line it serves and rate the fit: strong, weak, none. Be honest, because the four why-buys come from the strong fits, and a weak fit that gets promoted to a why-buy becomes a claim the product cannot keep. Gaps are findings, not failures; they tell product what to build and marketing what not to say.

## Stage 3: four why-buys

Cluster the strong fits. Each cluster that answers a top-ranked job, pain or gain is a candidate why-buy. Four is the target: enough to cover a product, few enough to hold in mind, and it forces the decision about what matters most. Three is fine for a narrow product; five means the decision has not been made.

Each why-buy must pass three tests: it answers something high on the ranked lists; the product can prove it with at least three features rather than adjectives; and it is distinct enough that no feature could sit under two of them. Name each as a short customer outcome, an adjective and a noun or a phrase in the customer's words, never a technology. Michelin makes tires; it sells safety. Find the safety.

Now the second interview. Show the four candidates with one line each on why they made the cut, and ask the user to argue: which one would their customer not recognise, which two overlap, which is missing. Revise until they agree. Only then stack.

## Stage 4: the messaging house

Build the table: four why-buys as column headers, and beneath each, every feature and spec that proves it, written as one sentence that carries both the proof and the benefit. Each fact from the intake lives in exactly one cell; if a fact seems to prove two columns, split it into its two distinct parts or decide which column owns it, and say so in the assumptions. Numbers stay exactly as supplied; never round, convert or improve them, and mark a missing number as needed rather than inventing one.

Price is a fact, not a reason: the number itself goes in a pricing strip or the parking lot, never in a column. A pricing model can be a why-buy when the customer profile ranks cost or commitment structure high and the model has at least three proofs of its own (per-use billing with no platform fee and no minimum; two days a week instead of a full-time hire). Say in the assumptions which of the two you decided.

Stacking is a two-way test. If a why-buy has fewer than three features beneath it, either the why-buy is wrong or the product has a gap; say which. If a feature fits nowhere, put it in a parking lot with a note. One or two parked features is normal. A long parking lot means the why-buys are wrong, so go back to stage 3. A feature the user loves that sits in the parking lot is a real finding: either the why-buys missed something, or the feature matters more to the company than to the customer, and the profile is the evidence for which.

The house is the document the team keeps. Use `assets/messaging-house-template.md` for the layout and save it as `messaging-house.md` unless asked otherwise. For a Word version, run `python scripts/export_docx.py messaging-house.md` once the markdown is final; it writes a black-on-white .docx beside it.

## Stage 5: value proposition and why-buy copy

Write the four why-buy sections first: a heading (the why-buy) and two paragraphs of 60 to 90 words. Paragraph one says what the customer gets, in their terms. Paragraph two proves it with the column's features, in order. This is the copy that becomes a homepage blade, a press release paragraph or a sales slide, so write it once and well.

Then the value proposition: a single paragraph of four or five sentences written from the four why-buys, addressed to the customer's biggest pain, promising the two or three biggest gains, naming the product exactly once, and ending on the emotional job in a sentence a person would actually say. This paragraph becomes the homepage intro. If it could be pasted above a competitor's product without changing a word, it is not done.

Write in the brand voice if one was given. Otherwise: short sentences, second person, concrete nouns, no stacked adjectives.

## Stage 6: channel copy

The default channel is the four-blade homepage: the value proposition as the intro, then four blades, one per why-buy, in column order, each built from its why-buy section, with the features beneath as proof. Produce other channels only when asked, and derive them from stages 4 and 5 rather than writing fresh. Any claim on any channel must point to a cell in the house; if it cannot, add the cell or cut the claim. `references/channel-assets.md` has the derivation for each channel.

## Output

Deliver `messaging-house.md` containing, in order: assumptions, customer profile, value map, why-buys with rationale, the house table and parking lot, why-buy copy, the value proposition, the channel copy requested, and open questions for the user. In chat, the first thing on screen is the four why-buys, then the value proposition, because that is what a person reacts to first. Any note about the fast track or assumptions comes after them, in one line. Then say where the file is and what to do with it next: share the house with the team as the one document to argue about, and derive every channel from it.

## Critique pass

Before delivering, reread as the customer and strike anything they would not care about. Reread as a competitor and check which why-buys they could also claim; sharpen the one that is most distinct. Check that the value proposition uses no feature that is not in the house, that the house uses no feature that is not in the intake, and that the product is named once in the value proposition. Then check the verbs: copy must describe a feature doing exactly what the intake says it does, not a natural extension of it. A lock that unlocks when your phone is near does not lock when you walk away unless the intake says so, and a tool that drafts a note does not file it. Extensions like these are the claims that get a web team in trouble. Then read the open questions against the copy: a question the user still has to answer must not be answered in the copy in either direction; the copy stays silent on it until they do. Finally, scan the house for any fact that appears in two cells. Fix what fails.

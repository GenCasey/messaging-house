# From value map to messaging house

Read this before stage 3. It covers choosing the four why-buys, the second interview, the two-way stacking test, how copy derives from the house, and a worked example.

## Why a messaging house

Strategy work usually arrives as a deck with no instructions for using it. The canvas ends with a list of pains relieved and gains created, and a copywriter still has to decide what to say first. The messaging house is the bridge. It reorganises the product's specifications so that every one of them supports one of four reasons to buy, and it becomes the one document every channel draws on. Once the house exists, web copy, PR and ads are translation jobs rather than invention jobs, and the team argues about the house instead of about sentences.

It is also a test of the product. Stacking engineering's spec list under four customer reasons shows quickly whether the specs line up to real selling motions. When they do not, that is a finding for product, and it is worth more than the copy.

## Choosing the four why-buys

Start from the ranked value map. Cluster the strong-fit lines. Each cluster that answers a top-ranked job, pain or gain is a candidate. Apply three tests.

Evidence: can the product prove it with at least three features or specs? One feature is a feature, not a why-buy. None is a wish.

Distinctness: could a feature sit under two candidates? If so they overlap; merge them or redraw.

Customer: would the customer recognise this as something they want, in their words? "Get paid without chasing" passes. "Automated AR workflows" asks the customer to translate.

Keep four, ordered by importance to the customer. That order becomes the order of blades on the homepage.

Name each why-buy as a short outcome: an adjective and a noun, or a phrase a customer would say. Avoid nouns only the company uses. Avoid superlatives the house cannot prove.

## The second interview

Show the four candidates with one line each on why they made the cut, then ask the user to argue with them: which would their customer not recognise, which two overlap, what is missing. This is the moment the workshop room used to move post-its around, and it is where a stranger's knowledge of their own customer corrects your draft. Revise until they agree, then stack. On the fast track, list the candidates and the rationale in the assumptions instead.

## Stacking, and the two-way test

Columns are the why-buys in order. Rows beneath are the features that prove each one, written as one sentence carrying proof and benefit together: "Reminders go out on the schedule you set, so late invoices chase themselves." Numbers stay exactly as supplied.

Stacking runs both ways. Sometimes a why-buy bends to fit what the product really does; sometimes the specs move until the columns hold. A column with fewer than three rows means the why-buy is wrong or the product has a gap; say which. A feature that fits nowhere goes to the parking lot with a note. A parking lot of one or two is normal; ten means the why-buys are wrong. A feature the user loves that ends up parked is a real finding: the why-buys missed something, or the feature matters more to the company than to the customer, and the profile says which.

## Deriving the copy

Each column expands into a why-buy section: heading plus two paragraphs, 120 to 180 words together. Paragraph one says what the customer gets. Paragraph two proves it with the rows, in order. This section is reused nearly verbatim as a homepage blade, a press release paragraph and a sales slide, which is why it is written once here.

The value proposition is written from the four why-buys as a single paragraph of four or five sentences: biggest pain, two or three biggest gains, product named exactly once, ending on the emotional job in a sentence a person would say. It becomes the homepage intro, and it should let a reader who reads nothing else know what the product is for and who it is for.

## Worked example: Ledgerline (fictional)

Ledgerline is an invented invoicing app for freelancers and studios of one to five people. The example is fictional so that the method, not the product, is the point.

Customer: Priya, who runs a two-person design studio and does invoicing on Sunday nights.

Jobs (top of the ranked list): get invoices out the same day the work ships (practical); get paid without chasing (practical); look organised to clients who are bigger than she is (social); stop dreading month end (emotional).

Pains: forgets which invoices are overdue; chasing feels awkward; clients pay by cheque or bank transfer and she reconciles by hand; fear that a missed invoice is money gone.

Gains: paid faster; nothing slips; knows at a glance what is owed; looks like a real company.

Spec list from engineering: invoice templates with logo; recurring invoices; automatic reminders on a schedule; card and bank-transfer payment links; bank feed that matches payments to invoices; late-fee rules; client portal showing balance and history; overdue dashboard; export to accounting software; two-user seats on the base plan.

Value map, strong fits: reminders and payment links answer "get paid without chasing"; bank feed and overdue dashboard answer "knows what is owed"; templates and client portal answer "look organised"; recurring invoices and same-day sending answer "get invoices out."

Four why-buys, after the second interview: Paid without chasing; Always know what you are owed; Look bigger than you are; Invoice in a minute. (The first draft had "Bank-grade reconciliation" as a why-buy; Priya would not recognise it, so it folded into the second column as proof.)

House, one column in full. Paid without chasing: reminders go out on the schedule you set, so late invoices chase themselves; every invoice carries a card and bank-transfer link, so paying takes one tap; late-fee rules apply automatically, so you never have the awkward conversation; the client portal shows the balance, so "did you get my invoice" answers itself.

Parking lot: two-user seats on the base plan (a pricing fact, not a reason to buy; belongs in the pricing section). Export to accounting software went under "Always know what you are owed."

Why-buy copy, the same column: You did the work; getting paid should not be a second job. Ledgerline sends the invoice, follows up on the days you choose, and adds the late fee you set, without you writing a single awkward email. Every invoice arrives with a payment link, so the client pays in one tap instead of finding their chequebook. When they ask whether you got their payment, the portal has already told them.

Value proposition: Invoicing is the part of running a studio nobody signed up for, and the part where money quietly goes missing. Ledgerline gets the invoice out the day the work ships, follows up so you do not have to, and shows you what you are owed in one screen. Clients pay in a tap and see their own balance, so you look like the bigger company in the room. Send it Friday, forget it, and spend Sunday night on something that is not a spreadsheet.

Four-blade homepage: the value proposition as the intro, then four blades in why-buy order, each with the heading, the first paragraph of the why-buy copy, and three or four rows as proof.

## The same house for other kinds of business

A service business: a residential electrician's why-buys might be "Passed first time," "On the day we said," "No surprise bill," "Safe for the next twenty years," with licences, inspection pass rate, fixed quotes and warranty terms as the rows. A consumer product: a cold-brew concentrate's why-buys might be "Café coffee in ten seconds," "No machine, no mess," "Tastes like the first cup all month," "Real beans, nothing else," with brew method, shelf life, serving count and ingredient list as rows. The method is the same: one real customer, jobs before features, four reasons, specs as proof.

## Signs the house is wrong

The why-buys read like an org chart (hardware, software, service, support). Rewrite around customer outcomes.

Two columns keep trading rows. Merge or redraw.

The value proposition mentions a feature that is not in the house. Add it or remove it.

The user's favourite feature is parked. Say why, with the profile as evidence.

# Value Proposition Canvas: jobs to be done, the questions, and the interview

Read this before stage 1. It covers the concept the user must understand before they can answer anything, the questions to ask, and how to run the first interview.

## Where the canvas comes from

The Value Proposition Canvas was developed by Alex Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur at Strategyzer. It expands two blocks of their Business Model Canvas, customer segments and value propositions, into two structured maps. On the right, the customer profile: jobs, pains, gains. On the left, the value map: products and services, pain relievers, gain creators. The exercise is to find fit between the two sides. This skill uses the canvas as its thinking tool and adds the writing Strategyzer leaves out.

## Jobs to be done: the explainer to give a cold user

Everything in this method stands on one idea, so give it to the user before asking them anything. In your own words, cover these points:

Start with one real person, not a segment. "Small business owners" is a demographic; "Priya, who runs a two-person design studio and does her invoicing on Sunday nights" is a customer. Demographics describe who someone is and say almost nothing about why they would buy.

Ask what that person is trying to get done at the moment they reach for the product. That is their job. Jobs come in three kinds and you want all three: practical (send the invoice, get paid), social (look professional to a client, look organised to a partner), emotional (stop dreading Sunday night, feel in control of the money). The emotional job is the one people least admit to and most pay for.

Ask about the doing, not the wanting. Customers are bad at telling you what they want; they describe a slightly better version of what they have. They are good at describing what they were trying to do and what got in the way.

The competition is whatever gets hired for the same job. Often that is not a product in the same category. It is a spreadsheet, a friend, doing nothing, or a bagel.

The milkshake story is the shorthand for this, and it is worth three sentences if the user has not heard it. A fast-food chain wanted to sell more milkshakes and asked customers what they wanted: thicker, chocolatier, cheaper. Bob Moesta's team, working with Clayton Christensen's framework, instead watched who bought them and when, and found that nearly half were sold before 8 a.m. to commuters driving alone, who had hired the milkshake to make a boring drive bearable with one hand. The real competitors were bananas, bagels and doughnuts, and the fixes that worked, thicker shake, thinner straw, fruit chunks, all served the job, not the flavour.

## The first interview

One question to open: "Tell me about one real customer, by name if you can, and what they were doing right before they used your product." If they cannot name one, ask who got the most out of the product this month.

From the answer, draft the jobs list yourself: eight to twelve items, practical, social and emotional, each a short phrase in context (the same task is a different job on a phone at a client's office than at a desk). Rank by how much the customer would pay to make the job disappear. Show it back and ask what is wrong with it. Correcting a draft is easier for a stranger than filling a blank canvas.

Then pains, then gains, the same way: draft from what you know, show, correct. Keep each exchange to one question.

Collect the spec list in this interview if you do not have it. Ask for the engineering version: every feature, setting, integration, limit and number. Marketing versions hide the proof you will need in the house.

## Customer jobs: questions when a list is thin

What is the customer trying to get done, and what does "done" look like to them?

How do they want to feel afterwards, and what do they have to do to feel that way?

What problem do they have that they do not know they have yet?

In what context does the job happen: alone or watched, under time pressure or not, on a phone or at a desk?

Which job would they pay to make disappear?

## Customer pains: questions when a list is thin

What makes the job feel bad: frustrations, annoyances, small daily tasks that add up?

What do they fear: financial, technical or social risk?

What mistakes do they make with what they use today, and how does that compound?

What does the current way cost them in time, money, effort or embarrassment?

What would make them look bad or feel they wasted money?

## Customer gains: questions when a list is thin

What makes their life easier: less to learn, fewer steps, lower cost, more reliable?

How do they measure a win: a number, a feeling, a reaction from someone?

Which savings do they talk about: time, money, effort?

What would they brag about to a colleague?

What would make them switch from what they use today?

## Building the value map

Mirror the profile line by line. Products and services answer jobs; pain relievers answer pains; gain creators answer gains. Write each answer against the profile item it serves and rate the fit: strong, weak, or none. Weak and missing fits are useful; they tell product what to build and tell marketing what not to claim.

## Common failures

Feature-crazy profiles: the jobs list is the feature list rewritten as verbs. Ask what the customer is trying to achieve after the feature has done its work.

Dead projects: a value map with no strong fits. Something intricate was built that only its makers wanted. Say so plainly; it is cheaper to hear now.

Persona soup: three customers blended into one, with contradictory jobs. Pick one and run the others separately.

## The same idea across kinds of business

The canvas does not care what you sell. A cold-brew concentrate is hired for "a good coffee in the time a pod takes, without a machine on the counter." A residential electrician is hired for "get the panel passed by the inspector without a second visit" and "not feel ripped off." An invoicing app is hired for "get paid without chasing" and "feel in control of the money." Practical, social, emotional, every time.
